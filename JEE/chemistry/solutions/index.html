Types of Solutions

A solution is a homogeneous mixture of two or more substances. The component present in the largest amount is called the solvent, and the component(s) present in smaller amounts are called solute(s).

1.1 Classification Based on Physical State

Type Solute State Solvent State Examples
Gas in Gas Gas Gas Air (O₂ in N₂)
Gas in Liquid Gas Liquid Aerated drinks (CO₂ in water)
Gas in Solid Gas Solid H₂ in Palladium
Liquid in Liquid Liquid Liquid Ethanol in water
Liquid in Solid Liquid Solid Mercury in gold (amalgam)
Solid in Gas Solid Gas Camphor in nitrogen
Solid in Liquid Solid Liquid Sugar in water, NaCl in water
Solid in Solid Solid Solid Alloys (brass, bronze)

1.2 Classification Based on Concentration

Saturated Solution

A solution that contains the maximum amount of solute that can dissolve in a given amount of solvent at a specific temperature.

Dynamic equilibrium exists between dissolved and undissolved solute.

Unsaturated Solution

A solution that contains less solute than the maximum amount that can dissolve at a specific temperature.

More solute can still be dissolved.

Supersaturated Solution

A solution that contains more solute than the saturated solution at a given temperature.

Unstable; crystallization occurs on disturbance.

💡 JEE Important Point

Binary Solutions: Solutions containing only two components (one solute + one solvent) are most commonly asked in JEE. Examples: NaCl in water, ethanol in water.

Concentration Terms

The concentration of a solution is a measure of the amount of solute dissolved in a given quantity of solvent or solution. Different concentration terms are used depending on the context.

2.1 Mass Percentage (w/w%)

Formula

\[\text{Mass percentage} = \frac{\text{Mass of solute}}{\text{Mass of solution}} \times 100\]

Example: 10 g NaCl in 90 g water → Mass % = (10/100) × 100 = 10%

2.2 Volume Percentage (v/v%)

Formula

\[\text{Volume percentage} = \frac{\text{Volume of solute}}{\text{Volume of solution}} \times 100\]

Example: 10 mL ethanol in 100 mL solution → Volume % = (10/100) × 100 = 10%

2.3 Molarity (M)

Definition & Formula

Number of moles of solute per liter of solution

\[\text{Molarity (M)} = \frac{\text{Moles of solute}}{\text{Volume of solution (L)}}\]
\[M = \frac{w_B \times 1000}{M_B \times V(\text{mL})}\]

where wB = mass of solute (g), MB = molar mass of solute, V = volume of solution

⚠️ Important Note

Molarity is temperature-dependent because volume changes with temperature. For precise work at different temperatures, use molality instead.

2.4 Molality (m)

Definition & Formula

Number of moles of solute per kilogram of solvent

\[\text{Molality (m)} = \frac{\text{Moles of solute}}{\text{Mass of solvent (kg)}}\]
\[m = \frac{w_B \times 1000}{M_B \times W_A(\text{g})}\]

where wB = mass of solute (g), MB = molar mass of solute, WA = mass of solvent (g)

2.5 Mole Fraction (χ)

Definition & Formula

Ratio of moles of one component to total moles of all components

\[\chi_A = \frac{n_A}{n_A + n_B}\]
\[\chi_B = \frac{n_B}{n_A + n_B}\]

Important: χA + χB = 1 (for binary solution)

2.6 Parts Per Million (ppm)

Formula

\[\text{ppm} = \frac{\text{Mass of solute}}{\text{Mass of solution}} \times 10^6\]

Used for: Very dilute solutions (water quality, air pollution)

📝 Solved Example 1

Question: Calculate molarity and molality of a solution containing 5.85 g of NaCl dissolved in 500 mL of solution (density = 1.02 g/mL).

Solution:

Step 1: Calculate moles of NaCl

\[\text{Molar mass of NaCl} = 23 + 35.5 = 58.5 \text{ g/mol}\] \[\text{Moles} = \frac{5.85}{58.5} = 0.1 \text{ mol}\]

Step 2: Calculate Molarity

\[M = \frac{0.1}{0.5} = 0.2 \text{ M}\]

Step 3: Calculate mass of solvent

\[\text{Mass of solution} = 500 \times 1.02 = 510 \text{ g}\] \[\text{Mass of solvent} = 510 - 5.85 = 504.15 \text{ g} = 0.50415 \text{ kg}\]

Step 4: Calculate Molality

\[m = \frac{0.1}{0.50415} = 0.198 \text{ m}\]

2.7 Interconversion of Concentration Terms

From → To Formula
Molarity → Molality m = (M × 1000)/(d × 1000 - M × M_B)
Molality → Molarity M = (m × d × 1000)/(1000 + m × M_B)
Mole fraction → Molality m = (χ_B × 1000)/(χ_A × M_A)
Molality → Mole fraction χ_B = (m × M_A)/(1000 + m × M_A)

Solubility

Solubility is the maximum amount of solute that can dissolve in a given amount of solvent at a specific temperature to form a saturated solution.

3.1 Factors Affecting Solubility

Temperature Effect

For Solids in Liquids:

  • Endothermic dissolution: Solubility ↑ with temperature ↑
  • Exothermic dissolution: Solubility ↓ with temperature ↑
  • Example: KNO₃ solubility increases, Ce₂(SO₄)₃ decreases

For Gases in Liquids:

  • Solubility ↓ with temperature ↑ (exothermic process)
  • Example: CO₂ solubility in cold drinks
Pressure Effect

For Solids/Liquids:

  • No significant effect (incompressible)

For Gases in Liquids:

  • Solubility ↑ with pressure ↑ (Henry's Law)
  • Example: CO₂ in soda bottles under pressure

💡 Le Chatelier's Principle

Dissolution equilibrium: Solute (s) ⇌ Solute (aq) + Heat

  • If dissolution is endothermic (Heat absorbed): Solubility ↑ with T ↑
  • If dissolution is exothermic (Heat released): Solubility ↓ with T ↑
4

Raoult's Law & Vapor Pressure

Raoult's Law relates the vapor pressure of a solution to the mole fraction of its components. It is fundamental to understanding ideal solutions and colligative properties.

4.1 Raoult's Law for Volatile Solute

Statement & Formula

For a solution of volatile components, the partial vapor pressure of each component is equal to the vapor pressure of the pure component multiplied by its mole fraction in the solution.

\[P_A = P_A^0 \times \chi_A\]
\[P_B = P_B^0 \times \chi_B\]
\[P_{\text{total}} = P_A + P_B = P_A^0 \chi_A + P_B^0 \chi_B\]

4.2 Raoult's Law for Non-Volatile Solute

Relative Lowering of Vapor Pressure

When a non-volatile solute is dissolved in a solvent, the vapor pressure of the solution is lower than that of pure solvent.

\[\frac{P_A^0 - P_A}{P_A^0} = \chi_B\]

where PA0 = vapor pressure of pure solvent, PA = vapor pressure of solution, χB = mole fraction of solute

📝 Solved Example 2

Question: The vapor pressure of pure water at 25°C is 23.76 mm Hg. What is the vapor pressure of a solution containing 10 g glucose (M = 180) in 200 g water?

Solution:

Step 1: Calculate moles

\[n_{\text{glucose}} = \frac{10}{180} = 0.0556 \text{ mol}\] \[n_{\text{water}} = \frac{200}{18} = 11.11 \text{ mol}\]

Step 2: Calculate mole fraction of glucose

\[\chi_{\text{glucose}} = \frac{0.0556}{0.0556 + 11.11} = 0.00498\]

Step 3: Apply Raoult's Law

\[\frac{P_0 - P_s}{P_0} = \chi_{\text{glucose}}\] \[P_s = P_0(1 - \chi_{\text{glucose}}) = 23.76 \times (1 - 0.00498)\]
\[P_s = 23.64 \text{ mm Hg}\]

4.3 Ideal vs Non-Ideal Solutions

Property Ideal Solution Non-Ideal Solution
Raoult's Law Obeys at all concentrations Shows deviation
ΔHmix = 0 ≠ 0 (+ or -)
ΔVmix = 0 ≠ 0 (+ or -)
A-B Interaction = A-A = B-B ≠ A-A or B-B
Examples Benzene-Toluene, n-hexane-n-heptane Ethanol-Water, Acetone-Chloroform

4.4 Positive and Negative Deviations

Positive Deviation

Characteristics:

  • Ptotal > Pideal (Raoult's law)
  • A-B interaction < A-A, B-B
  • ΔHmix > 0 (endothermic)
  • ΔVmix > 0 (volume increases)

Examples:

  • Ethanol + Cyclohexane
  • Acetone + Carbon disulfide
  • Ethanol + Water
Negative Deviation

Characteristics:

  • Ptotal < Pideal (Raoult's law)
  • A-B interaction > A-A, B-B
  • ΔHmix < 0 (exothermic)
  • ΔVmix < 0 (volume decreases)

Examples:

  • Acetone + Chloroform
  • Chloroform + Ether
  • HNO₃ + Water

⚠️ JEE Important

Azeotropes: Constant boiling mixtures that cannot be separated by simple distillation.

  • Minimum boiling azeotrope: Positive deviation (95% ethanol + 5% water)
  • Maximum boiling azeotrope: Negative deviation (68% HNO₃ + 32% water)

Colligative Properties

Colligative properties depend only on the number of solute particles in solution, not on their identity. These are crucial for JEE with high weightage in numerical problems.

5.1 Relative Lowering of Vapor Pressure

Formula

\[\frac{P_0 - P_s}{P_0} = \chi_{\text{solute}} = \frac{n_B}{n_A + n_B}\]

For dilute solutions: nA >> nB

\[\frac{P_0 - P_s}{P_0} \approx \frac{n_B}{n_A} = \frac{w_B \times M_A}{M_B \times w_A}\]

5.2 Elevation of Boiling Point

Definition & Formula

The boiling point of a solution is higher than that of the pure solvent.

\[\Delta T_b = T_b - T_b^0 = K_b \times m\]

where Kb = molal elevation constant (ebullioscopic constant), m = molality

\[K_b = \frac{R \times M_A \times (T_b^0)^2}{1000 \times \Delta H_{\text{vap}}}\]

💡 Important Kb Values

Water:

Kb = 0.52 K kg mol⁻¹

Benzene:

Kb = 2.53 K kg mol⁻¹

Chloroform:

Kb = 3.63 K kg mol⁻¹

Ethanol:

Kb = 1.22 K kg mol⁻¹

5.3 Depression of Freezing Point

Definition & Formula

The freezing point of a solution is lower than that of the pure solvent.

\[\Delta T_f = T_f^0 - T_f = K_f \times m\]

where Kf = molal depression constant (cryoscopic constant), m = molality

\[K_f = \frac{R \times M_A \times (T_f^0)^2}{1000 \times \Delta H_{\text{fusion}}}\]

📝 Solved Example 3

Question: Calculate the freezing point of a solution containing 3.42 g of sucrose (M = 342) in 100 g water. Kf for water = 1.86 K kg mol⁻¹.

Solution:

Step 1: Calculate molality

\[\text{Moles of sucrose} = \frac{3.42}{342} = 0.01 \text{ mol}\] \[m = \frac{0.01}{0.1} = 0.1 \text{ m}\]

Step 2: Calculate ΔTf

\[\Delta T_f = K_f \times m = 1.86 \times 0.1 = 0.186 \text{ K}\]

Step 3: Calculate freezing point

\[T_f = 273.15 - 0.186 = 272.964 \text{ K} = -0.186°\text{C}\]

5.4 Osmotic Pressure

Definition & Formula

The minimum pressure required to prevent osmosis (flow of solvent into solution through semipermeable membrane).

\[\pi = CRT = \frac{n_B}{V}RT\]

where π = osmotic pressure, C = molarity, R = 0.0821 L atm K⁻¹ mol⁻¹, T = temperature (K)

\[\pi = \frac{w_B \times R \times T}{M_B \times V}\]

⚠️ Why Osmotic Pressure is Most Sensitive?

Among all colligative properties, osmotic pressure is the most sensitive and is preferred for determining molar mass of macromolecules (proteins, polymers) because:

  • It is measured at room temperature (no heating required)
  • Even dilute solutions show measurable osmotic pressure
  • Other colligative properties show very small changes for large molecules

📝 Solved Example 4

Question: A solution of 2 g of a protein in 100 mL water shows an osmotic pressure of 0.0821 atm at 300 K. Calculate the molar mass of protein.

Solution:

Using formula:

\[\pi = \frac{w_B \times R \times T}{M_B \times V}\]

Rearranging:

\[M_B = \frac{w_B \times R \times T}{\pi \times V}\]

Substituting values:

\[M_B = \frac{2 \times 0.0821 \times 300}{0.0821 \times 0.1}\]
\[M_B = 6000 \text{ g/mol}\]

5.5 Isotonic, Hypertonic & Hypotonic Solutions

Type Osmotic Pressure Effect on RBC Example
Isotonic π₁ = π₂ (equal) No change (normal) 0.9% NaCl (saline)
Hypertonic π₁ > π₂ (higher) Shrinks (crenation) Sea water
Hypotonic π₁ < π₂ (lower) Swells and bursts (hemolysis) Distilled water

5.6 Comparison of Colligative Properties

Property Formula Used For Sensitivity
RLVP (P₀-Ps)/P₀ = χ_B Small molecules Low
ΔTb ΔT_b = K_b × m Molar mass determination Medium
ΔTf ΔT_f = K_f × m Molar mass determination Medium
π π = CRT Macromolecules, proteins High (most sensitive)

Henry's Law

Henry's Law describes the solubility of gases in liquids and states that at constant temperature, the solubility of a gas in a liquid is directly proportional to the partial pressure of the gas above the liquid.

6.1 Statement and Formula

Mathematical Expression

\[P = K_H \times \chi\]

where P = partial pressure of gas, KH = Henry's constant, χ = mole fraction of gas in solution

\[\text{or } m = K_H \times P\]

m = molality (another form), where different KH value is used

💡 Important Points about KH

  • Higher KH value → Lower solubility of gas
  • Lower KH value → Higher solubility of gas
  • KH increases with temperature (solubility decreases)
  • KH is different for different gas-solvent pairs

6.2 Applications of Henry's Law

🥤 Soft Drinks & Soda

CO₂ is dissolved in soft drinks under high pressure. When bottle is opened, pressure decreases, solubility decreases, and CO₂ escapes causing fizz.

🤿 Scuba Diving

At high underwater pressure, more N₂ dissolves in blood. Rapid ascent causes N₂ bubbles to form (decompression sickness/"the bends"). Divers use He-O₂ mixture as He has lower solubility.

🏔️ High Altitude Effects

At high altitudes, partial pressure of O₂ is low, so less O₂ dissolves in blood. This causes breathing difficulty (anoxia). Over time, body produces more RBCs to compensate.

🐟 Aquatic Life

At higher temperatures, less O₂ dissolves in water. This can kill aquatic animals. Also, thermal pollution from factories increases water temperature, reducing O₂ solubility.

📝 Solved Example 5

Question: KH for CO₂ in water at 298 K is 1.67 × 10⁸ Pa. Calculate the solubility of CO₂ in water at 298 K under 1 atm CO₂ pressure. (1 atm = 1.013 × 10⁵ Pa)

Solution:

Using Henry's Law:

\[P = K_H \times \chi\] \[\chi = \frac{P}{K_H} = \frac{1.013 \times 10^5}{1.67 \times 10^8}\]
\[\chi_{\text{CO}_2} = 6.07 \times 10^{-4}\]

This means mole fraction of CO₂ in water is 6.07 × 10⁻⁴ at 1 atm pressure.

⚠️ Limitations of Henry's Law

  • Applicable only for dilute solutions
  • Gas should not react chemically with solvent
  • Gas should not associate or dissociate in solution
  • Valid only at moderate temperature and pressure

Abnormal Molecular Mass & van't Hoff Factor

When solutes undergo association or dissociation in solution, the observed molar mass differs from the theoretical value. This is explained using the van't Hoff factor (i).

7.1 van't Hoff Factor (i)

Definition

van't Hoff factor (i) is the ratio of:

\[i = \frac{\text{Observed colligative property}}{\text{Calculated colligative property}}\]
\[i = \frac{\text{Normal molar mass}}{\text{Abnormal molar mass}}\]
\[i = \frac{\text{Total number of particles after dissociation/association}}{\text{Number of formula units initially dissolved}}\]

7.2 Modified Colligative Property Formulas

Property Original Formula Modified Formula (with i)
RLVP (P₀-Ps)/P₀ = χ_B (P₀-Ps)/P₀ = i × χ_B
Elevation of BP ΔT_b = K_b × m ΔT_b = i × K_b × m
Depression of FP ΔT_f = K_f × m ΔT_f = i × K_f × m
Osmotic Pressure π = CRT π = iCRT

7.3 Dissociation (i > 1)

When Electrolytes Dissociate

If a solute dissociates into n ions with degree of dissociation α:

\[i = 1 + (n-1)\alpha\]

Examples:

NaCl → Na⁺ + Cl⁻ (n = 2)

Complete dissociation (α = 1): i = 1 + (2-1) × 1 = 2

CaCl₂ → Ca²⁺ + 2Cl⁻ (n = 3)

Complete dissociation (α = 1): i = 1 + (3-1) × 1 = 3

K₄[Fe(CN)₆] → 4K⁺ + [Fe(CN)₆]⁴⁻ (n = 5)

Complete dissociation (α = 1): i = 1 + (5-1) × 1 = 5

7.4 Association (i < 1)

When Solutes Associate

If n molecules associate with degree of association α:

\[i = 1 - \left(1 - \frac{1}{n}\right)\alpha\]

Example:

Benzoic acid in benzene: 2C₆H₅COOH → (C₆H₅COOH)₂

Dimerization (n = 2), if α = 0.5:

\[i = 1 - \left(1 - \frac{1}{2}\right) \times 0.5 = 1 - 0.25 = 0.75\]

📝 Solved Example 6

Question: A 0.1 m aqueous solution of NaCl freezes at -0.372°C. Calculate the van't Hoff factor and degree of dissociation of NaCl. (Kf for water = 1.86 K kg mol⁻¹)

Solution:

Step 1: Calculate theoretical ΔTf

\[\Delta T_f(\text{theoretical}) = K_f \times m = 1.86 \times 0.1 = 0.186 \text{ K}\]

Step 2: Observed ΔTf

\[\Delta T_f(\text{observed}) = 0 - (-0.372) = 0.372 \text{ K}\]

Step 3: Calculate van't Hoff factor

\[i = \frac{\Delta T_f(\text{observed})}{\Delta T_f(\text{theoretical})} = \frac{0.372}{0.186} = 2\]

Step 4: Calculate degree of dissociation

For NaCl: n = 2 (Na⁺ + Cl⁻)

\[i = 1 + (n-1)\alpha\] \[2 = 1 + (2-1)\alpha\] \[\alpha = 1\]
\[\text{van't Hoff factor } i = 2\] \[\text{Degree of dissociation } \alpha = 1 \text{ (100% dissociation)}\]

💡 Quick Reference: i Values

Substance Behavior i (theoretical)
Glucose, Urea (non-electrolyte) No dissociation i = 1
NaCl, KCl AB → A⁺ + B⁻ i = 2
MgCl₂, Na₂SO₄ AB₂ → A²⁺ + 2B⁻ i = 3
FeCl₃, K₃PO₄ AB₃ → A³⁺ + 3B⁻ i = 4
Benzoic acid (in benzene) Association (dimer) i < 1

Solutions - Complete Guide for JEE 2025-26

Why Solutions is Important for JEE?

Solutions is a high-weightage chapter in JEE Chemistry, carrying 8-12% weightage in both JEE Main and Advanced. Key reasons for importance:

  • Colligative Properties - Most scoring topic with direct numerical questions
  • van't Hoff Factor - Frequently appears in JEE Advanced
  • Raoult's Law - Combined with chemical equilibrium in advanced problems
  • Numerical Intensive - Quick solving skills can save 10+ minutes in exam

This chapter has direct applications in chemical kinetics, electrochemistry, and thermodynamics making it foundational for Physical Chemistry.

Key Formulas to Remember

1. Concentration Terms

M = n/V(L), m = n/W(kg), χ = n₁/(n₁+n₂)

2. Colligative Properties

  • • ΔTb = i × Kb × m
  • • ΔTf = i × Kf × m
  • • π = iCRT
  • • (P₀-Ps)/P₀ = i × χB

3. van't Hoff Factor

i = 1 + (n-1)α (dissociation)

i = 1 - (1-1/n)α (association)

4. Raoult's & Henry's Law

P = P° × χ, P = KH × χ

⚠️ Common Mistakes to Avoid in JEE Exam

  • ❌
    Confusing molarity with molality: Molarity depends on temperature (volume changes), molality doesn't. Use molality for colligative properties.
  • ❌
    Forgetting van't Hoff factor: Always multiply colligative property formulas by 'i' for electrolytes (NaCl, CaCl₂, etc.)
  • ❌
    Wrong KH interpretation: Higher KH means LOWER solubility (inverse relationship)
  • ❌
    Unit conversion errors: Always convert to proper units - temperature to Kelvin, volume to liters, mass to kg for molality
  • ❌
    Isotonic solution confusion: Equal osmotic pressure (π₁ = π₂) not equal concentration
