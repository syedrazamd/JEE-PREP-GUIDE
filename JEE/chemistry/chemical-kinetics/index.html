Chemical Kinetics

Master the rates, mechanisms, and factors influencing chemical reactions for JEE success!

Last Updated: October 27, 2023

1. Introduction to Chemical Kinetics

Chemical Kinetics is the branch of physical chemistry concerned with the study of reaction rates, the factors that affect them, and the molecular mechanism by which reactions occur. Unlike thermodynamics, which tells us if a reaction is feasible, kinetics tells us how fast it will proceed.

For JEE, understanding chemical kinetics is vital for solving problems related to reaction speed, predicting product formation over time, and comprehending the influence of various parameters like temperature and concentration. This chapter lays the foundation for many real-world chemical processes.

2. Rate of a Chemical Reaction

The rate of a reaction quantifies how quickly reactants are consumed and products are formed. It is defined as the change in concentration of a reactant or product per unit time.

2.1. Average Rate vs. Instantaneous Rate

  • Average Rate: The change in concentration over a measurable time interval.
    Average Rate = -Δ[Reactant] / Δt = +Δ[Product] / Δt
    The negative sign for reactants indicates their concentration decreases over time.
  • Instantaneous Rate: The rate of reaction at a specific moment in time. It is determined by calculating the slope of the tangent to the concentration vs. time curve at that instant.
    Instantaneous Rate = -d[Reactant] / dt = +d[Product] / dt

2.2. Units of Reaction Rate

The standard unit for reaction rate is mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹ or M s⁻¹ (molarity per second).

2.3. Stoichiometry and Rate Expression

For a general balanced chemical reaction: aA + bB → cC + dD

The rate of reaction is expressed as:

Rate = - (1/a) d[A]/dt = - (1/b) d[B]/dt = + (1/c) d[C]/dt = + (1/d) d[D]/dt

This ensures the rate is unique, irrespective of which reactant/product's concentration change is monitored.

3. Rate Law and Rate Constant

The Rate Law (or Rate Equation) is an experimentally determined expression that relates the rate of a reaction to the concentration of reactants. It is not necessarily derived from the stoichiometric coefficients of the balanced equation.

For a reaction: A + B → Products

General Rate Law Expression:

Rate = k[A]x[B]y
  • k is the Rate Constant (also known as specific reaction rate). It is a proportionality constant that is unique for a given reaction at a specific temperature.
  • x and y are the orders of reaction with respect to reactants A and B, respectively. They are experimentally determined and can be integers, fractions, or zero.
  • The overall order of reaction is the sum of the individual orders: n = x + y.

The unit of the rate constant k depends on the overall order of the reaction. For an nth order reaction, the unit of k is (mol L⁻¹)1-n s⁻¹ or M1-n s⁻¹.

4. Order of a Reaction

The order of a reaction is an experimentally determined value that describes how the rate of a reaction depends on the concentration of reactants.

4.1. Zero-Order Reactions (n=0)

The rate of reaction is independent of the concentration of the reactant(s).

  • Rate Law: Rate = k[A]⁰ = k
  • Integrated Rate Law:
    [A] = [A]₀ - kt
    ([A]₀ = initial concentration, [A] = concentration at time t)
  • Half-life (t½):
    t½ = [A]₀ / 2k

    For zero-order reactions, t½ is directly proportional to the initial concentration.

  • Examples: Decomposition of ammonia on a hot platinum surface, certain enzymatic reactions at high substrate concentrations.

4.2. First-Order Reactions (n=1)

The rate of reaction is directly proportional to the first power of the concentration of the reactant.

  • Rate Law: Rate = k[A]¹
  • Integrated Rate Law:
    ln[A] = ln[A]₀ - kt OR ln([A]₀/[A]) = kt
    Or, in common log form:
    k = (2.303/t) log([A]₀/[A])
  • Half-life (t½):
    t½ = ln(2) / k = 0.693 / k

    For first-order reactions, t½ is independent of the initial concentration – a key characteristic.

  • Examples: Radioactive decay, decomposition of N₂O₅, hydrolysis of esters in acidic medium (pseudo-first order).

4.3. Second-Order Reactions (n=2)

The rate of reaction is proportional to the second power of a single reactant's concentration or the product of the first power of two reactants' concentrations.

  • Rate Law: Rate = k[A]² or Rate = k[A][B]
  • Integrated Rate Law (for Rate = k[A]²):
    1/[A] = 1/[A]₀ + kt
  • Half-life (t½):
    t½ = 1 / (k[A]₀)

    For second-order reactions, t½ is inversely proportional to the initial concentration.

4.4. Pseudo-First-Order Reactions

These are reactions that appear to be of the first order but are actually of a higher order. This happens when one of the reactants is present in a very large excess, so its concentration remains essentially constant throughout the reaction. The overall rate then depends only on the concentration of the other reactant. Example: Hydrolysis of sucrose in the presence of dilute acid.

5. Molecularity of a Reaction

Molecularity is a theoretical concept defined as the number of reacting species (atoms, ions, or molecules) that must collide simultaneously in an elementary reaction to bring about a chemical change.

  • Unimolecular: One reacting species. Molecularity = 1. E.g., decomposition of N₂O₅.
  • Bimolecular: Two reacting species. Molecularity = 2. E.g., dissociation of HI.
  • Trimolecular (Termolecular): Three reacting species. Molecularity = 3. Rare due to very low probability of three species colliding simultaneously.

5.1. Distinction Between Order and Molecularity

Feature Order of Reaction Molecularity of Reaction
Definition Sum of powers of concentration terms in the rate law (experimentally determined). Number of species colliding in an elementary step (theoretical concept).
Value Can be zero, integer, or a fraction. Always a positive integer (1, 2, or 3).
Applicability For elementary and complex reactions. Only for elementary reactions. For complex reactions, it refers to individual elementary steps.
Relation Experimentally determined and cannot be predicted from a balanced equation. A theoretical concept, derived from the mechanism of an elementary step.

6. Integrated Rate Laws (Summary)

Integrated rate laws provide a relationship between reactant concentrations and time, allowing for calculation of concentration at any given time or the time required for a certain change.

Order Differential Rate Law Integrated Rate Law Half-life (t½) Unit of 'k'
Zero (n=0) Rate = k [A] = [A]₀ - kt [A]₀ / 2k M s⁻¹
First (n=1) Rate = k[A] ln([A]₀/[A]) = kt 0.693 / k s⁻¹
Second (n=2) Rate = k[A]² 1/[A] = 1/[A]₀ + kt 1 / (k[A]₀) M⁻¹ s⁻¹

7. Temperature Dependence: Arrhenius Equation

The rate of nearly all chemical reactions increases with an increase in temperature. The Arrhenius equation provides a quantitative relationship between the rate constant and temperature.

Arrhenius Equation:

k = A * e-Ea/RT
  • k = Rate constant
  • A = Arrhenius factor or pre-exponential factor (frequency factor), representing the frequency of collisions.
  • Ea = Activation energy (the minimum energy required for molecules to react).
  • R = Gas constant (8.314 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹)
  • T = Absolute temperature (in Kelvin)

7.1. Graphical Determination of Ea

Taking the natural logarithm of the Arrhenius equation yields a linear form:

ln k = ln A - Ea/RT

Plotting ln k against 1/T gives a straight line with:

  • Slope = -Ea/R
  • Y-intercept = ln A

7.2. Effect of Temperature on Rate Constant (Two-Point Form)

If rate constants k₁ and k₂ are known at two different temperatures T₁ and T₂ respectively, activation energy can be calculated:

ln(k₂/k₁) = (Ea/R) * (1/T₁ - 1/T₂)
Or, using common logarithm:
log(k₂/k₁) = (Ea / 2.303R) * ( (T₂ - T₁) / (T₁T₂) )

8. Half-life Period (t½)

The half-life period (t½) of a reaction is the time required for the concentration of a reactant to decrease to half of its initial value.

  • Zero-Order: t½ = [A]₀ / 2k (Directly proportional to initial concentration)
  • First-Order: t½ = 0.693 / k (Independent of initial concentration)
  • Second-Order (for Rate = k[A]²): t½ = 1 / (k[A]₀) (Inversely proportional to initial concentration)
  • General Formula (for n-th order, n≠1):
    t½ = (2n-1 - 1) / ( (n-1)k[A]₀n-1 )
    This formula is useful for determining reaction order if multiple half-lives at different initial concentrations are known.

9. Catalysis (Brief)

A catalyst is a substance that changes the rate of a chemical reaction without undergoing any net chemical change itself. Catalysts primarily function by providing an alternative reaction mechanism with a lower activation energy, thus increasing the reaction rate.

  • Positive Catalyst: Increases reaction rate by lowering activation energy.
  • Negative Catalyst (Inhibitor): Decreases reaction rate by increasing activation energy.
  • Homogeneous Catalysis: Catalyst and reactants are in the same phase.
  • Heterogeneous Catalysis: Catalyst and reactants are in different phases.
  • Enzyme Catalysis: Biological catalysts (enzymes) are highly specific proteins that accelerate biochemical reactions.

Catalysts do not change the equilibrium constant of a reversible reaction; they only help in attaining equilibrium faster.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ) on Chemical Kinetics for JEE

Q1: What is the primary focus of Chemical Kinetics?

Chemical Kinetics primarily focuses on studying the rates of chemical reactions, the factors that influence these rates (like concentration, temperature, catalyst), and the step-by-step mechanism by which a reaction proceeds.

Q2: How is the order of a reaction different from its molecularity?

Order of reaction is an experimentally determined value that can be zero, integer, or a fraction, representing the sum of the powers of concentration terms in the rate law. It applies to both elementary and complex reactions. Molecularity is a theoretical concept, always a positive integer (1, 2, or 3), referring to the number of reacting species in an elementary step, and is only applicable to elementary reactions.

Q3: Explain the significance of the Arrhenius equation.

The Arrhenius equation (k = A * e-Ea/RT) mathematically describes the temperature dependence of reaction rates. It shows that the rate constant (k) increases exponentially with temperature and decreases exponentially with activation energy (Ea). This equation is crucial for predicting how reaction rates change with temperature and for calculating activation energy.

Q4: Why is the half-life of a first-order reaction constant?

For a first-order reaction, the half-life (t½ = 0.693 / k) depends only on the rate constant (k), which is constant at a given temperature. It does not depend on the initial concentration of the reactant. This means that no matter how much reactant is present initially, it will always take the same amount of time for half of it to be consumed.

Q5: How does a catalyst speed up a reaction without being consumed?

A catalyst accelerates a reaction by providing an alternative reaction pathway or mechanism that has a lower activation energy (Ea) than the uncatalyzed reaction. By lowering Ea, a larger fraction of reactant molecules possess sufficient energy to react, thereby increasing the reaction rate. The catalyst is regenerated at the end of the reaction, so its concentration remains unchanged.
