1. Introduction to Chemical Kinetics

Chemical Kinetics is the branch of physical chemistry concerned with understanding the rates of chemical reactions. It's not enough to know if a reaction will happen (which thermodynamics tells us); we also need to know how fast it will happen and by what pathway. This knowledge is crucial for optimizing industrial processes, understanding biological systems, and predicting environmental changes.

In this chapter, we will explore various aspects of chemical kinetics, including defining reaction rates, understanding the factors that influence them, deriving rate laws, and applying concepts like activation energy and collision theory to explain reaction mechanisms.

2. Rate of a Chemical Reaction

The rate of a chemical reaction is defined as the change in concentration of a reactant or product per unit time. It can be expressed in terms of the disappearance of a reactant or the appearance of a product.

2.1 Average Rate vs. Instantaneous Rate

  • Average Rate: The change in concentration over a measurable time interval. \[ \text{Average Rate} = - \frac{\Delta[\text{Reactant}]}{\Delta t} = + \frac{\Delta[\text{Product}]}{\Delta t} \]
  • Instantaneous Rate: The rate of reaction at a specific moment in time. This is obtained by taking the limit of the average rate as \(\Delta t \to 0\). \[ \text{Instantaneous Rate} = - \frac{d[\text{Reactant}]}{dt} = + \frac{d[\text{Product}]}{dt} \]

2.2 Stoichiometry and Rate Expression

For a general reaction: \(aA + bB \to cC + dD\), the rate of reaction is related to the rate of change of concentration of reactants and products by their stoichiometric coefficients:

Rate Expression

\[ \text{Rate} = - \frac{1}{a} \frac{d[A]}{dt} = - \frac{1}{b} \frac{d[B]}{dt} = + \frac{1}{c} \frac{d[C]}{dt} = + \frac{1}{d} \frac{d[D]}{dt} \]

The unit of reaction rate is typically mol L-1 s-1 or M s-1.

Example 1: Rate Calculation

For the reaction \(2N_2O_5(g) \to 4NO_2(g) + O_2(g)\), the concentration of \(N_2O_5\) decreases from 0.5 M to 0.4 M in 30 minutes. Calculate the average rate of reaction, the rate of formation of \(NO_2\), and the rate of formation of \(O_2\).

Solution:

Given: \( \Delta[N_2O_5] = (0.4 - 0.5) \text{ M} = -0.1 \text{ M} \)
\( \Delta t = 30 \text{ minutes} = 30 \times 60 \text{ s} = 1800 \text{ s} \)

1. Average rate of reaction:

\[ \text{Rate} = - \frac{1}{2} \frac{\Delta[N_2O_5]}{\Delta t} = - \frac{1}{2} \frac{(-0.1 \text{ M})}{1800 \text{ s}} = \frac{0.1}{3600} \text{ M s}^{-1} = 2.78 \times 10^{-5} \text{ M s}^{-1} \]

2. Rate of formation of \(NO_2\): From stoichiometry, \( \text{Rate} = + \frac{1}{4} \frac{d[NO_2]}{dt} \implies \frac{d[NO_2]}{dt} = 4 \times \text{Rate} \)

\[ \frac{d[NO_2]}{dt} = 4 \times (2.78 \times 10^{-5} \text{ M s}^{-1}) = 1.112 \times 10^{-4} \text{ M s}^{-1} \]

3. Rate of formation of \(O_2\): From stoichiometry, \( \text{Rate} = + \frac{d[O_2]}{dt} \)

\[ \frac{d[O_2]}{dt} = 2.78 \times 10^{-5} \text{ M s}^{-1} \]

3. Factors Affecting Reaction Rate

Several factors can influence how fast a chemical reaction proceeds:

  • Concentration of Reactants: Generally, an increase in reactant concentration leads to an increase in reaction rate because there are more molecules available to collide and react.
  • Temperature: Most reactions speed up with an increase in temperature, as molecules gain more kinetic energy, leading to more frequent and energetic collisions (discussed in Arrhenius equation).
  • Presence of a Catalyst: Catalysts are substances that alter the rate of a reaction without being consumed. They typically speed up reactions by providing an alternative reaction pathway with a lower activation energy.
  • Surface Area of Reactants: For heterogeneous reactions (reactants in different phases, e.g., solid and gas), increasing the surface area of solid reactants increases the rate of reaction.
  • Nature of Reactants: The inherent chemical properties of reactants, such as bond strengths and molecular complexity, influence how easily they react.
  • Presence of Light (Radiation): Some reactions (photochemical reactions) are initiated or accelerated by light absorption.

4. Rate Law and Rate Constant

The rate law (or rate expression) is a mathematical equation that relates the rate of a reaction to the concentrations of its reactants. For a general reaction \(aA + bB \to cC + dD\), the experimentally determined rate law is typically of the form:

Rate Law

\[ \text{Rate} = k[A]^x[B]^y \]
  • \(k\): Rate Constant - A proportionality constant that is characteristic of a given reaction at a particular temperature. Its value depends on temperature and the specific reaction, but not on reactant concentrations.
  • \(x\): Order with respect to A - The exponent of the concentration term of A in the rate law.
  • \(y\): Order with respect to B - The exponent of the concentration term of B in the rate law.
  • Overall Order of Reaction: The sum of the exponents of the concentration terms in the rate law (\(x+y\)).

Important: The exponents \(x\) and \(y\) are NOT necessarily equal to the stoichiometric coefficients \(a\) and \(b\). They must be determined experimentally.

Example 2: Determining Rate Law & Order

Consider the reaction \(A + B \to C\). The following data were obtained at 298 K:

Experiment [A] (M) [B] (M) Initial Rate (M/s)
1 0.10 0.10 4.0 × 10-5
2 0.20 0.10 8.0 × 10-5
3 0.10 0.20 1.6 × 10-4

Determine the rate law, the order of reaction with respect to A and B, and the overall order of the reaction.

Solution:

Assume the rate law is \( \text{Rate} = k[A]^x[B]^y \).

1. Determine order with respect to A (\(x\)):
Compare Exp. 1 and Exp. 2, where [B] is constant.

\[ \frac{\text{Rate}_2}{\text{Rate}_1} = \frac{k[A]_2^x[B]_2^y}{k[A]_1^x[B]_1^y} \implies \frac{8.0 \times 10^{-5}}{4.0 \times 10^{-5}} = \left(\frac{0.20}{0.10}\right)^x \left(\frac{0.10}{0.10}\right)^y \] \[ 2 = (2)^x \implies x=1 \]
So, the reaction is first order with respect to A.

2. Determine order with respect to B (\(y\)):
Compare Exp. 1 and Exp. 3, where [A] is constant.

\[ \frac{\text{Rate}_3}{\text{Rate}_1} = \frac{k[A]_3^x[B]_3^y}{k[A]_1^x[B]_1^y} \implies \frac{1.6 \times 10^{-4}}{4.0 \times 10^{-5}} = \left(\frac{0.10}{0.10}\right)^x \left(\frac{0.20}{0.10}\right)^y \] \[ 4 = (2)^y \implies y=2 \]
So, the reaction is second order with respect to B.

3. Rate Law:

\[ \text{Rate} = k[A]^1[B]^2 \text{ or } \text{Rate} = k[A][B]^2 \]

4. Overall Order: \(x+y = 1+2 = 3\). The overall order is third order.

5. Order and Molecularity

5.1 Order of Reaction

As discussed, the order of a reaction (or reaction order) is the sum of the powers to which the concentration terms are raised in the experimentally determined rate law.

  • It can be an integer (0, 1, 2, 3), a fraction, or even negative.
  • It is determined experimentally and cannot be predicted from the balanced equation.

5.2 Molecularity of Reaction

Molecularity refers to the number of reacting species (atoms, ions, or molecules) that collide simultaneously in an elementary reaction to bring about a chemical reaction.

  • It is always a positive integer (1, 2, or 3 for unimolecular, bimolecular, and termolecular reactions, respectively).
  • It applies only to elementary reactions (single-step reactions). For complex reactions (multi-step), molecularity has no meaning for the overall reaction, but each elementary step has its own molecularity.
  • It cannot be fractional or zero.

5.3 Difference between Order and Molecularity

Feature Order of Reaction Molecularity of Reaction
Definition Sum of exponents of concentration terms in the experimentally determined rate law. Number of reacting species in an elementary step.
Origin Experimental concept. Theoretical concept, derived from reaction mechanism.
Value Can be 0, integer, or fraction, positive or negative. Always a positive integer (1, 2, or 3).
Applicability Applies to elementary and complex reactions (overall reaction). Applies only to elementary reactions.
Relation to Stoichiometry Not necessarily equal to stoichiometric coefficients. Equal to stoichiometric coefficients for elementary reactions.

6. Integrated Rate Laws

Differential rate laws describe how the rate of reaction depends on reactant concentrations. Integrated rate laws provide a relationship between reactant concentrations and time, which is more useful for practical applications.

6.1 Zero-Order Reactions

A reaction is zero order if its rate is independent of the reactant concentration.

Zero-Order Rate Law

Differential Rate Law:

\[ \text{Rate} = - \frac{d[A]}{dt} = k \]

Integrated Rate Law:

\[ [A]_t = [A]_0 - kt \]

Half-life (t½):

\[ t_{1/2} = \frac{[A]_0}{2k} \]

Characteristics: Rate is constant. Half-life depends on initial concentration. Plot of \([A]\) vs. \(t\) is a straight line with slope \(-k\).

6.2 First-Order Reactions

A reaction is first order if its rate is directly proportional to the concentration of one reactant.

First-Order Rate Law

Differential Rate Law:

\[ \text{Rate} = - \frac{d[A]}{dt} = k[A] \]

Integrated Rate Law (in terms of natural log):

\[ \ln[A]_t = \ln[A]_0 - kt \quad \text{or} \quad \ln\left(\frac{[A]_0}{[A]_t}\right) = kt \]

Integrated Rate Law (in terms of common log):

\[ \log[A]_t = \log[A]_0 - \frac{kt}{2.303} \quad \text{or} \quad \log\left(\frac{[A]_0}{[A]_t}\right) = \frac{kt}{2.303} \]

Half-life (t½):

\[ t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{k} = \frac{0.693}{k} \]

Characteristics: Rate depends on concentration. Half-life is independent of initial concentration. Plot of \(\ln[A]\) vs. \(t\) is a straight line with slope \(-k\).

6.3 Pseudo-First-Order Reactions

Sometimes, a reaction which is not truly first order behaves as a first-order reaction. This happens when one of the reactants is present in a large excess, so its concentration change during the reaction is negligible. For example, the hydrolysis of an ester in the presence of water:

\[ CH_3COOC_2H_5 + H_2O \xrightarrow{H^+} CH_3COOH + C_2H_5OH \] \[ \text{Rate} = k'[CH_3COOC_2H_5][H_2O] \]
If \(H_2O\) is in large excess, \([H_2O]\) remains almost constant. So, \(k'[H_2O] = k_{eff}\) (a new pseudo-rate constant), and the rate law becomes:
\[ \text{Rate} = k_{eff}[CH_3COOC_2H_5] \]
This is a pseudo-first-order reaction.

Example 3: First-Order Reaction Time

A first-order reaction has a rate constant of \(5.5 \times 10^{-14} \text{ s}^{-1}\) at 300 K. How long will it take for the reactant concentration to decrease from 0.1 M to 0.025 M?

Solution:

For a first-order reaction, the integrated rate law is:

\[ \ln\left(\frac{[A]_0}{[A]_t}\right) = kt \]
Given: \(k = 5.5 \times 10^{-14} \text{ s}^{-1}\), \([A]_0 = 0.1 \text{ M}\), \([A]_t = 0.025 \text{ M}\).
\[ \ln\left(\frac{0.1}{0.025}\right) = (5.5 \times 10^{-14} \text{ s}^{-1}) \times t \] \[ \ln(4) = (5.5 \times 10^{-14} \text{ s}^{-1}) \times t \] \[ 1.386 = (5.5 \times 10^{-14} \text{ s}^{-1}) \times t \] \[ t = \frac{1.386}{5.5 \times 10^{-14} \text{ s}^{-1}} = 2.52 \times 10^{13} \text{ s} \]

7. Half-life of a Reaction

The half-life (\(t_{1/2}\)) of a reaction is the time required for the concentration of a reactant to decrease to one-half of its initial concentration.

7.1 Half-life for Different Orders

  • Zero-Order: \(t_{1/2} = \frac{[A]_0}{2k}\) (depends on initial concentration).
  • First-Order: \(t_{1/2} = \frac{0.693}{k}\) (independent of initial concentration).
  • Second-Order (for \(2A \to P\)): \(t_{1/2} = \frac{1}{k[A]_0}\) (depends on initial concentration).

Example 4: Half-life and Concentration

The half-life period of a first-order reaction is 60 minutes. What percentage of the reactant will remain after 180 minutes?

Solution:

For a first-order reaction, the half-life is constant. Number of half-lives in 180 minutes = \( \frac{180 \text{ min}}{60 \text{ min/half-life}} = 3 \text{ half-lives} \).

After 1st half-life: 50% remains.
After 2nd half-life: 50% of 50% = 25% remains.
After 3rd half-life: 50% of 25% = 12.5% remains.

Alternatively, using integrated rate law:

\[ k = \frac{0.693}{t_{1/2}} = \frac{0.693}{60 \text{ min}} = 0.01155 \text{ min}^{-1} \] \[ \ln\left(\frac{[A]_0}{[A]_t}\right) = kt = (0.01155 \text{ min}^{-1}) \times (180 \text{ min}) = 2.079 \] \[ \frac{[A]_0}{[A]_t} = e^{2.079} \approx 8 \] \[ \frac{[A]_t}{[A]_0} = \frac{1}{8} = 0.125 \]
Percentage remaining = \(0.125 \times 100\% = 12.5\%\).

8. Temperature Dependence - Arrhenius Equation

The Arrhenius equation quantitatively describes the effect of temperature on the rate constant (\(k\)) of a reaction:

Arrhenius Equation

\[ k = Ae^{-E_a/RT} \]
  • \(k\): Rate constant
  • \(A\): Arrhenius factor or pre-exponential factor (frequency factor). It represents the frequency of collisions.
  • \(E_a\): Activation energy (in J/mol or kJ/mol). The minimum energy required for reactant molecules to transform into products.
  • \(R\): Gas constant (8.314 J mol-1 K-1)
  • \(T\): Absolute temperature (in Kelvin)

8.1 Effect of Temperature on Rate Constant

Taking the natural logarithm of the Arrhenius equation:

\[ \ln k = \ln A - \frac{E_a}{RT} \]
This is in the form \(y = mx + c\). A plot of \(\ln k\) vs. \(1/T\) gives a straight line with slope \(-E_a/R\) and y-intercept \(\ln A\).

8.2 Arrhenius Equation at Two Temperatures

If \(k_1\) and \(k_2\) are the rate constants at temperatures \(T_1\) and \(T_2\) respectively:

Arrhenius Equation for Two Temperatures

\[ \ln\left(\frac{k_2}{k_1}\right) = \frac{E_a}{R}\left(\frac{1}{T_1} - \frac{1}{T_2}\right) \]

Example 5: Activation Energy Calculation

The rate constant of a reaction doubles when its temperature changes from 298 K to 308 K. Calculate the activation energy (\(E_a\)) for the reaction. (Given \(R = 8.314 \text{ J mol}^{-1} \text{ K}^{-1}\)).

Solution:

Given:
\(T_1 = 298 \text{ K}\)
\(T_2 = 308 \text{ K}\)
Let \(k_1 = k\), then \(k_2 = 2k\) (since rate constant doubles).

Using the Arrhenius equation for two temperatures:

\[ \ln\left(\frac{k_2}{k_1}\right) = \frac{E_a}{R}\left(\frac{1}{T_1} - \frac{1}{T_2}\right) \] \[ \ln\left(\frac{2k}{k}\right) = \frac{E_a}{8.314 \text{ J mol}^{-1} \text{ K}^{-1}}\left(\frac{1}{298 \text{ K}} - \frac{1}{308 \text{ K}}\right) \] \[ \ln(2) = \frac{E_a}{8.314}\left(\frac{308 - 298}{298 \times 308}\right) \] \[ 0.693 = \frac{E_a}{8.314}\left(\frac{10}{91784}\right) \] \[ E_a = \frac{0.693 \times 8.314 \times 91784}{10} \] \[ E_a = 52897.6 \text{ J mol}^{-1} \approx 52.9 \text{ kJ mol}^{-1} \]

9. Collision Theory of Chemical Reactions

Collision theory explains how chemical reactions occur and why reaction rates differ for different reactions.

9.1 Postulates of Collision Theory

  • Collisions are Necessary: Reactant molecules must collide with each other for a reaction to occur.
  • Activation Energy: Not all collisions lead to a reaction. Only those collisions where the colliding molecules possess energy greater than or equal to a minimum energy called activation energy (\(E_a\)) are effective.
  • Proper Orientation: The colliding molecules must also have the proper orientation relative to each other at the moment of collision to break existing bonds and form new ones.

The rate of reaction according to collision theory is given by:

\[ \text{Rate} = Z_{AB}e^{-E_a/RT} \]
Where \(Z_{AB}\) is the collision frequency (number of collisions per unit volume per unit time) between reactants A and B, and \(e^{-E_a/RT}\) is the fraction of molecules having energy equal to or greater than \(E_a\).

9.2 Steric Factor (\(P\))

Collision theory sometimes overestimates reaction rates, especially for complex molecules. This is because it doesn't fully account for the orientation factor. The steric factor \(P\) (or probability factor) is introduced to correct this:

\[ k = P Z_{AB}e^{-E_a/RT} \quad \text{ (Modified Arrhenius Equation based on Collision Theory)} \]
The factor \(P\) accounts for the proper orientation of molecules during collision. Its value is typically between 0 and 1, reflecting that not all energetic collisions lead to product formation.

10. Catalysis

A catalyst is a substance that increases the rate of a chemical reaction without itself being consumed in the overall reaction. It does this by providing an alternative reaction pathway with a lower activation energy.

10.1 Types of Catalysis

  • Homogeneous Catalysis: Reactants and catalyst are in the same phase (e.g., all gas or all liquid). Example: Acid hydrolysis of sucrose.
  • Heterogeneous Catalysis: Reactants and catalyst are in different phases (e.g., solid catalyst in a gas-phase reaction). Example: Haber process (iron catalyst for ammonia synthesis).

10.2 Characteristics of Catalysts

  • They change reaction rates but do not alter the equilibrium constant (they speed up both forward and reverse reactions equally).
  • A small amount of catalyst can catalyze a large amount of reactants.
  • They are often specific in their action (e.g., enzyme catalysis).
  • They do not initiate a reaction but rather accelerate existing ones.

Previous Year Questions Analysis (JEE Main & Advanced)

Chemical Kinetics is a highly scoring chapter for JEE. Questions typically involve direct application of formulas or graphical interpretation.

  • Weightage: Expect 1-2 questions in JEE Main and occasionally 1 question in JEE Advanced (can be part of a multi-concept problem).
  • Repeated Question Types:
    • Calculations involving integrated rate laws (especially first-order) to find time, concentration, or rate constant.
    • Half-life calculations for zero and first-order reactions, often involving multiple half-lives.
    • Determining reaction order and rate constant from experimental initial rate data.
    • Arrhenius equation problems: calculating activation energy from two rate constants at different temperatures, or predicting rate constant at a new temperature.
    • Graphical questions related to integrated rate laws (\([A]\) vs. \(t\), \(\ln[A]\) vs. \(t\)).
    • Conceptual questions distinguishing between order and molecularity, or properties of catalysts.
  • Key Focus Areas: Integrated rate laws (derivation and application), half-life concept, Arrhenius equation, and experimental determination of rate law are crucial.

Practice with PYQs from the last 5-7 years is highly recommended to build speed and accuracy.

Practice Problem Set

Test your understanding with these practice problems across different difficulty levels.

Level 1: Basic Understanding

  • Define molecularity and order of reaction. Can they be equal? Give an example.
  • A reaction \(A \to B\) is first order. If initial concentration of A is 0.2 M and after 10 minutes, it is 0.1 M, what is the half-life of the reaction?
  • For a zero-order reaction, how does the half-life change if the initial concentration is doubled?

Level 2: Application & Calculation

  • The decomposition of \(N_2O_5\) (g) follows a first-order reaction. \(k = 6.2 \times 10^{-4} \text{ s}^{-1}\) at 320 K. What is the half-life of \(N_2O_5\)? How much time will it take to reduce the initial concentration of \(N_2O_5\) to one-third?
  • For a reaction, the activation energy is 75 kJ/mol. If the rate constant at 298 K is \(1.5 \times 10^{-4} \text{ s}^{-1}\), what will be the rate constant at 313 K?
  • The reaction \(2NO(g) + O_2(g) \to 2NO_2(g)\) follows the rate law: \( \text{Rate} = k[NO]^2[O_2] \). What is the overall order of the reaction? If the concentration of NO is doubled and \(O_2\) is halved, how does the reaction rate change?

Level 3: Advanced & Multi-Concept

  • A reaction is second order with respect to reactant A and first order with respect to reactant B. When the concentrations of A and B are 0.1 M and 0.2 M respectively, the initial rate is \(4.0 \times 10^{-5} \text{ M s}^{-1}\). Calculate the rate constant. What would be the rate if concentrations of both A and B are halved?
  • The decomposition of a compound follows a first-order kinetics. If it takes 15 minutes for 20% of the compound to decompose, calculate the time required for 60% decomposition.
  • Draw and explain the energy profile diagram for an exothermic reaction in the presence and absence of a catalyst.
